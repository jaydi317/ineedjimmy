The Circle

You already do this. Every time you're at your best, you're doing it by feel.
This is just the name for it — so you can do it on purpose.

Read this part even if you read nothing else

There's a difference between being powerful and being unstoppable.

Powerful is what you already are. You get the moments — the room goes quiet, the woman signs up before you've pitched anything, you say a thing you didn't plan and she starts crying. But those moments happen to you. You can't schedule them. You can't hand them to a coach on your team.

Unstoppable is when you can aim.

Everything below is one idea that turns a thing you do by instinct into a thing you can point at a webinar, a pitch, a client on a Tuesday afternoon, or a letter.

And here's the actual reason it matters — not the marketing reason. Every person walking around has a story-shaped expectation inside them about how a life is supposed to go: something calls you, you're scared, someone helps you, it costs you something, you come back different. When a woman is stuck, it is almost always because she got called and never went. She's living inside a paused movie.

What you do for a living is press play.

This is the map of where the pause button is.

Part One

The forever part

Read once. Keep it. It applies to everything you will ever say to a woman.

The whole thing in one sentence

Every story human beings have ever told is the same story — and it's the same story because it's the shape of what actually happens to a person when they change.

A guy named Joseph Campbell spent his career reading myths. Greek, African, Japanese, Native American, Bible stories, fairy tales. Thousands of them, from cultures separated by oceans that had never heard of each other.

They were all telling the same story.

The same pattern. Different costume.

Your words. But that's exactly what he found.

He didn't invent anything — he noticed something. And the reason every culture on earth independently landed on the same story is that it was never a story structure at all. It's the shape of a human being changing. Stories didn't invent it. Stories copied it off of life.

Which is why it works on people. It's not a trick. It's a description.

The picture — in your words instead of the big ones

How to read it: the horizontal line is the only thing that really matters. Above it is the life she already knows — safe, familiar, not working. Below it is everything she hasn't done yet.

She starts at the top and goes clockwise. The orange arrow is the moment everything hinges on: she says yes out loud and can't unsay it. Before the arrow she's deciding. After it she's changing.

The dark scribble at the bottom isn't decoration. That's where the woman she's been for thirty years stops being in charge. It's the worst part and it's the whole point.

Why it works on people

She has already heard this story five thousand times. The Wizard of Oz. Moana. Eat Pray Love. Every makeover show. Every before-and-after picture she has ever cried at.

She doesn't know she knows it. But the horse knows it cold.

So when you talk to her in this shape — on a webinar, on a call, in a letter — she isn't being sold. She's hearing a song she already knows the words to. Her body leans in before her brain decides anything.

You're not building a road. You're driving on one that's already there.

That's the honest answer to why this is powerful. It isn't clever. It's pre-installed.

The one spot almost everybody is stuck on

Look at the circle again. Right after the tap on the shoulder, before the line, there's a station that says "she says not yet."

That scene is in every single version of the story. Luke tells Obi-Wan no. Moana turns the boat around. Dorothy tries to go home before anything starts. The hero always refuses first. If it's missing, the story feels fake.

So the woman who's been on your list for four years — opening every email, watching every live, never buying — is not a flake and not a bad lead.

She's on schedule.

She is standing exactly where the story says she should be standing. And that means the thing she needs from you is not more information. More information at the "not yet" makes it worse — it gives her something new to study instead of something to cross.

What she needs is the next scene. And the next scene is always the same scene.

The next scene: somebody who's already been there shows up

Gandalf. Glinda. Gramma Tala. Mr. Miyagi. And notice the timing — the helper never arrives at the beginning, and never arrives after she's already gone. She arrives at the "not yet." That's the entire job.

That's the role you're playing, whether you meant to or not. But there are two ways to play it and only one of them works:

The finished one

Already made it. Has it figured out. Standing at the top of the mountain going "come up here, I'll fix you."

She admires you — and doesn't move. Admiration creates distance, and distance is exactly what "you don't know what it's like to be me" is made out of.

The one who's still walking

"I'm crossing my own line right now. Come with me."

That's not a guru. That's company. A woman will walk ten times farther with company than she will toward a summit.

You are naturally the second one. That's your whole gift. Most people in this business spend a decade trying to fake it.

What this actually unlocks for you

This is the part I most want you to have. Not for the letter — for everything after it.

Once you can see the circle, you can locate any woman on it in about ninety seconds, and where she's standing tells you exactly what she needs. Which means you stop guessing.

On a webinar

Stop thinking of it as teaching with a pitch bolted on the end. It's one motion: here's where you are → here's the thing that tapped you → here's why you said not yet, and why that's normal → here's me, crossing my own line, right now → come with me. The offer stops being a gear change. It's just the next sentence.

On a sales call

The only question that matters is where is she on the circle. Everything else follows. You already do this by feel — this just tells you what you're feeling for.

One-on-one with a woman — and this is the big one

When a client is in the dark part at the bottom, she does not experience it as transformation. She experiences it as failure. "I did so good for six weeks and then I ate the entire thing." That's the moment she quits, and she quits because of shame, not because of food.

If you can look at her and say "this is exactly where you're supposed to be — this is the part where the old you fights to stay alive, and it only shows up because it's working" — you just turned the worst moment of her program into evidence that it's working.

You converted her shame into proof.

That's not marketing. That's the difference between a client who finishes and a client who disappears in month three. It's retention, it's testimonials, it's referrals — and it's the most useful thing on this whole page.

On yourself

You're mid-circle right now. You know you are. Knowing the shape means you can tell the difference between something's wrong with me and I'm at the part where it's supposed to hurt.

The words you never say out loud

Never use these in front of anybody: hero's journey, threshold, archetype, atonement, revelation, collective unconscious, Joseph Campbell.

You were right on the audio — they're too big. They're too big for everyone, which is why nobody who actually uses this says them. The second a person can see the machinery, the machinery stops working.

This is a lens you look through. It is not a thing you talk about. Throw every one of those words away and keep the picture.

Part Two

The right-now part

Just the Inner Circle letter. You don't need Part One perfect to use this.

The frame you already handed me

This wasn't my idea. It was yours — I just recognized it. You said something like:

"When I started the first Inner Circle, I pleaded with women to let me help them. I kept trying to convince them they needed me. Well, as we're winding down, I want to share a huge breakthrough I had… I actually need you just as much."

That's not a nice opening line. That's you being the second kind of helper — on the page, in real time, while they watch. It does four things at once and none of them look like selling:

  1. Nobody argues with a confession. Her guard comes up against a claim. There's no claim here. Nothing to push against.
  2. It proves you're actually on the journey. You're not telling them to be vulnerable — you're being vulnerable while they read. They conclude on their own that it's safe with you, which means they never have to take your word for it. People sometimes believe what they're told. They never doubt what they conclude.
  3. It flips the power without costing you an ounce of authority. "You guys are giants" only lands from somebody who obviously went somewhere. Brass balls alone is intimidating. Open heart alone is soft. Same paragraph, and they'll follow you anywhere.
  4. It makes it an invitation instead of a transaction. "Come with me" is a completely different sentence than "buy nine more months."

Nobody wakes up wanting to purchase nine more months of coaching.
Everybody wakes up wanting to go somewhere.

Three rules while you write it

  1. Objections are the refusal scene, not obstacles. "Too expensive." "I've tried everything." "Not a good time." "Who am I to think I could?" That's the script — every hero says those lines. Don't argue with them. Say the fear out loud before she does, then hand her what makes it survivable.
  2. The payment IS the line. The moment money moves is the moment she can't unsay yes. So the ask has to feel like stepping over something, not checking out of a cart.
  3. Tell the truth about the hard part. Say in advance that it gets hard and the old her will fight. Two things happen: she trusts you immediately because she knows it's true, and when it gets hard in month three she doesn't quit — because you already told her that part was coming.

The only thing to keep in your head

When you sit down to write, don't think "how do I convince them."

She's standing at her line. I'm standing at mine.
What do I say so we cross together?

Everything after that is just words — and words are the easy part.
