# STRATEGY — Two Independent Concepts for Administrator Assistance

*Prepared by Fable · Private draft for Steven Wittenauer · Not deployed*

---

## The job to be done in the first three seconds

An Indiana school-board member or superintendent lands on the homepage. Within a breath they should know:

1. This is for me.
2. Administrator Assistance solves the kind of hard week I am currently having.
3. What they actually do is *put an experienced person on the problem*.
4. These are trusted, credible, Indiana public-school people.
5. The next step is a single confidential call.

Both concepts are engineered around that job. They differ in **what they lead with as the product**.

---

## <a id="concept-a"></a>Concept A · The Bench

### Strategic idea

> **The product is the people.** Administrator Assistance is an on-call bench of proven Indiana school leaders — a private-client advisory firm that a school board can trust with the district's hardest week.

The homepage puts the *humans* in the hero. A visible bench of six anonymized former leaders — Interim Superintendent, Business Manager, Elementary Principal, Curriculum Director, HS Principal / Search Lead, Transportation / Operations — sits inside the first viewport as the central visual. The headline is intentionally human: *"When a district faces a problem it has never solved before, we send someone who has."*

### Design language

- **Typography:** editorial serif (Iowan Old Style / Palatino) for headlines; system sans-serif for chrome, labels, and numbers.
- **Palette:** deep navy `#0d1b2a`, cream `#f5efe4`, brass `#a37a2c`. Warm and substantial, not corporate.
- **Structure:** three narrative chapters replace the traditional service menu — *People, on short notice · Money, plans, and negotiations · Operations that quietly matter.*
- **Proof:** three case notes rendered as *Before → The Match → After.*

### Why it works for Steven

- It rewards the thing Steven actually built for eighteen years: a real bench of real people.
- It reads as calm, experienced, and human — the qualities public-sector leaders trust.
- It gives school-board members a "someone would show up" feeling before they read a single service name.
- A retired superintendent would forward it to a peer without embarrassment.

### Where Concept A is deliberately restrained

- The Indiana coverage map is not on the homepage — coverage is not the lead.
- No overt "we are large" language. Scale is shown through the bench and the 50+ / 1,500+ / 190+ trio.
- No aggressive CTAs. The single CTA is *"Request a Confidential Call."*

## <a id="concept-b"></a>Concept B · The Coverage Map

### Strategic idea

> **The product is the statewide network.** Administrator Assistance is Indiana's district-response network — a civic institution that has already been to most of these districts and can respond to the next one within the week.

The homepage puts the *state* in the hero. A stylized Indiana silhouette with 40+ ochre dots (districts served) and 4 cardinal-red dots with halos (recent engagements) sits next to a bold sans-serif headline: *"The experienced answer to a district problem you have never faced before."* The 50+ / 1,500+ / 190+ numbers run as a full-width banner directly beneath the fold.

### Design language

- **Typography:** bold sans-serif (system-ui / Segoe / Helvetica) throughout — civic and institutional.
- **Palette:** deep charcoal `#111417`, parchment `#f6f4ef`, cardinal `#a01d1d`, ochre `#c58a2b`.
- **Structure:** the service menu is replaced by a **four-step Response Pathway** (Intake → Match → Deploy → Hand-off) with explicit timelines, plus a **Capacity Index** table that groups all services under four situational headers.
- **Proof:** three engagement cards structured as *Trigger / Match / Outcome.*

### Why it works for Steven

- It shows the *reach* Steven has quietly built — 190+ Indiana districts — without a single boastful sentence.
- It gives buyers a mental picture of a statewide utility they can call, not a boutique firm they have to hope has capacity.
- The four-step pathway makes the calendar promise concrete: "same day" / "24–72 hours" / "within the week."
- A superintendent could hand this to a skeptical board member and win them over on the strength of the map alone.

### Where Concept B is deliberately restrained

- The map is clearly labeled "stylized illustration" — no false precision.
- The cardinal red is used sparingly: title strip, one headline underline, one CTA. It signals urgency without shouting.
- The founder letter appears late on the page, not first — the network is the hero, the founder is the guarantor.

## Recommendation

**Lead with Concept A · The Bench.** Two reasons:

1. **It matches what Steven actually sells.** Districts do not hire a network — they hire the person who walks through the door on Tuesday. Concept A puts that person in the hero.
2. **It is the concept most likely to make Steven say "that is what I have been trying to explain."** The tone is exactly the tone a retired superintendent uses when describing his own company — calm, human, understated, substantial.

**Then borrow the best of Concept B.** Specifically:
- Move the four-step pathway diagram from Concept B onto Concept A's page as a supporting section (it makes the calendar concrete).
- Add a scaled-down Indiana coverage map to Concept A as a section on the way to the CTA (it does the "we have been here before" work in a single image).

If Steven's interview reveals that he most wants school boards outside his personal network to find him cold, invert the recommendation: **Concept B leads, Concept A becomes the "Who We Are" page.** Concept B's map is the better ad, and cold visitors need the ad first.

- Whether same-day/next-day placement is a promise the company makes, or only a public example.

- Which named districts he could feature by name, on the record, with district permission.

## The five interview answers most likely to change the final website

1. **"Where do good projects actually come from?"** If the answer is "referral almost exclusively," the homepage should reward the referred visitor with fast recognition and a single confidential call — Concept A wins. If the answer includes cold search or state-level RFPs, Concept B's coverage map earns its place as the hero.

2. **"Which two or three services create most of the business?"** The service architecture on both concepts should be re-ranked so those two or three services occupy the first row / first chapter. Everything else recedes.

3. **"What still has to pass through you personally that should not?"** This determines whether the primary CTA is "Request a call" (routes to Steven) or "Request a match" (routes to a matching process). If Steven is the bottleneck, the site should intentionally *reduce* inbound cold calls, not maximize them.

4. **"What would you change first with a blank check and five excellent people?"** If the answer is *find and package more work like the last five great engagements*, the site should feature those engagements by name — with district permission. If the answer is *succession / less dependence on me*, the site should quietly promote the bench as the durable asset and de-emphasize Steven as the single point of failure.

5. **"What do outsiders always misunderstand?"** Whatever Steven names in the last 30 seconds of the interview goes directly above the fold. That sentence, in his voice, is likely to be the strongest headline either concept could ever carry.
