The Local AI Meetup Model

How a free monthly meetup in your own city becomes a front-end offer that fills a $999 assessment and a $1–2K/mo retainer — with no list, no ads, and no content.

Source: Corey Ganim — 3 videos, 4,579 words transcribed Prepared for Jason Gazaway By Jarvis, for Jimmy Davis
In one line: Every direct-response marketer is fighting for attention on a screen. This model wins by being the only person in town who shows up in a room — a free monthly “AI for Business” meetup that functions as a live, recurring, zero-cost front-end offer to a market that still buys from handshakes.

The 8 Insights

Insight 01

The meetup is the front door. It is not the business.

The free monthly meetup exists for one reason: to put you in a room with non-technical local business owners and earn trust in 90 minutes that content couldn’t buy in six months. The money is on the back end — a paid assessment and a monthly retainer.

In Kennedy terms: this is a lead-generation magnet with a live human delivery mechanism. The event is the ad. The room is the list.
Insight 02

The market is defined by who it excludes.

Every city has AI meetups — for developers. Nobody is running one for the 55-year-old owner of a $4M HVAC company who knows he’s falling behind and has no idea where to start. Naming it “AI for Business” does the qualifying before anyone walks in.

These people live in local business Facebook groups, Chamber meetings, and BNI-style networking circles. They are not going to buy from a Meta ad or a YouTube video. They will buy from the person who ran the room.

Why it matters: a starving crowd with no competing supplier. The technical crowd is oversupplied; the owner crowd is unserved.
Insight 03

Everyone selling AI is hiding behind a screen — that’s the arbitrage.

AI made cold email and content free, so everyone does it, so owners tune all of it out. The scarce asset became the exact thing AI can’t fake: being physically in the room. You can build more trust in 30 seconds face-to-face than in six months of DMs.

The positioning claim: nobody in your city has taken the title of “the local AI person.” It is unclaimed, and it is claimed by whoever shows up monthly for a year.
Insight 04

The offer ladder is three rungs and it’s already built.

RungWhat it isPrice

The assessment tells them what. The concierge does it with them. Classic ascension — diagnostic sold as a product, then continuity.
Insight 05

The unit economics of one evening.

Assessment: $999 for roughly 2 hours of fulfillment ≈ $500/hr. Concierge: $1–2K/mo for 1.5 hours of calls ≈ $667–$1,333/hr. Close one assessment plus one retainer from a single meetup and that evening produced $2,000–$3,000; two assessments plus a retainer, $3,000–$4,000.

Honest flag: in a separate clip he states the concierge rate as $750–$1,250/hr, which doesn’t reconcile with the $667–$1,333 above — he’s allocating delivery time differently. Both statements are in his own words. These are his claims and examples, not audited numbers.
Insight 06

The highest-margin line item costs nothing to deliver.

“Unlimited Voxer access” — a walkie-talkie app. The client hears an AI expert in my pocket, any time I want one. That perception is what justifies the top of the price range. The reality, in his words: they barely use it.

The principle: price against perceived access, not against delivered hours. This is the same lever as Kennedy’s “gold-level” membership tier.
Insight 07

You don’t need an audience. You borrow one.

The single move that took his event from 12 people to 30: co-hosting with someone who already owns the local audience. He approached the owner of a 38,000-member Charlotte business-owner Facebook group with a no-brainer pitch — venue’s booked, material’s built, all you do is show up and tell your people — and expects 50+ at the next one.

Underneath that: post value-first (never a naked pitch) in five or more local groups, link in the comments to dodge no-promo rules. His first event ever was 12 people, all driven by one realtor who invited her office.

The ask that works: you bring expertise, they bring the audience, they get co-host status. Zero cash changes hands.
Insight 08

It compounds like SEO — which is exactly why most people quit.

Month one produces a trickle. Month six runs 10x. Attendees are locally well-connected, so they start bringing their own people, and referrals from other AI people in the room outpace the direct leads. He reports getting more referrals than leads from event one.

So he locked a permanent date — last Thursday of every month — and intends to never miss one for five years.

The three ways it dies: low turnout at event one (fix: co-host), pitching too hard (fix: teach 15–20 min, then a 30–60 second CTA), and hosting once then quitting (fix: lock the recurring date before event one).

The Run of Show

  1. Lock a recurring date and a venue. A local co-working space. Last Thursday monthly, permanently.
  2. Post value-first in 5–10 local business Facebook groups. Lead with a question — “what’s the one thing you wish AI could handle in your business? Drop it below and I’ll cover it live.” Event link in the comments.
  3. DM one local group owner about co-hosting. This is the highest-leverage 10 minutes in the whole model.
  4. Capture contact info at the door or through a registration link. No email or phone, no funnel.
  5. Teach one simple thing for 15–20 minutes, screen-shared, zero jargon — no APIs, no MCPs. His last one was 15 minutes on building Claude skills.
  6. Close with a 30–60 second CTA as the room winds down. Offer the paid personalized assessment. Say you’ll follow up individually. Don’t sell in the room — sell on the call.
  7. Text every attendee within 24 hours. “Did you enjoy it? Anything I can help you with when it comes to AI?” That’s the whole follow-up.

The final blink

This is not an AI play. It’s a 1980s direct-response play — free seminar, live demonstration, diagnostic offer, continuity — pointed at the one topic every local business owner is anxious about and nobody local has claimed.

The reason the window is open is not that the tactic is clever. It’s that booking a room and posting in five Facebook groups takes about thirty minutes, and almost nobody will do it.
